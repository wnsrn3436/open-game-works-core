# commercial quality

What commercial games share whatever their genre, platform and art style, and what amateur or AI-made work misses. It is not a style: the look of each game (its forms, materials, palette, type, motion and sound) comes from its own theme (`## art direction` and `## audio direction` of `design.md`) and from its reference games (`records/setup/reference/reference.md`: the `## presentation game` for how the screens are laid out and staged, the `## game` for the world, characters, effects, motion and sound; the UI is drawn in the hand of the world). A quality below says what must be true; the theme and the reference games say how it looks.

## how a game meets it

- Before making a screen or an asset of a kind (a title, a pause screen, a result, a HUD, an enemy, a floor, an icon, a hit effect), the agent that makes it looks at the same kind in the reference videos with `ogw frames`: a screen in the videos of the presentation game, the world, characters, effects and motion in those of the reference game (the moments of `records/setup/reference/reference.md`, and other stretches of the videos). It makes that kind the way that game does it, in this game's own theme. When the videos never show that kind, it is made from the theme and the qualities below, in the forms of the nearest kind they show (e.g. a settings screen in the forms of the pause screen)
- Judge an asset where it is seen: in a capture of the game at play size, next to the main character and over its real background, not on an asset sheet. An asset that looks fine alone can fail in the game (e.g. a character drawn as a standalone illustration and shrunk into the scene)
- Rendering fidelity is not the bar. Crude but consistent work meets these qualities; polished but generic work fails them
- A `Check:` is answered yes when the screen meets it, and only where it applies to what the screen shows

## qualities of every resource

1. One hand: every resource (UI, characters, environment, props, effects, icons, logo, title art, app icon) is made in the same hand as the game's world: its line or pixel scale, shading, palette and motifs. The HUD, pause, popups, settings and notifications may instead be plain type and flat shapes in the game's palette. Check: next to the world art, are the icons, the effects and the UI of the other screens drawn with its line, shading and palette, and does no UI have a bevel, gloss or outline that the world's characters do not have (a raised button on a portrait phone screen excepted)?
2. Made for how it is seen: an asset is designed for the size and place it is shown at: a few big shapes and a clear silhouette at play size, fine detail only where the screen can show it. UI is drawn with no more detail than the world. Check: at the size shown, is every UI part and icon drawn with no more detail than the world art, with nothing that turns to noise?
3. Attention is directed: what the player must notice stands out by value, hue or a mark nothing else uses; the play surface and the dressing stay quiet; a screen that presents one thing has one focal element, and a screen for choosing among many (settings, an inventory, a shop, a level list) shows its focused item instead; ornament and strong signals are kept for a few places and rare, important moments. Check: on a menu or a popup, is ornament kept to the one or two places that should draw the eye (e.g. the logo, the main action, a card), not spread over the screen (a frame, a band of char or curl, deep tears, wax or a seal added to every sheet and button counts as spread; an edge no rougher or darker than the edges of the presentation game's own sheets, cards or panels is the material itself)?
4. Identity from the theme: places, characters, UI and type come from the game's own theme, not from genre defaults (a specific place, a character's own hook, the game's name drawn as a logo). Check: can you name what on the screen comes from this game's theme rather than from its genre?
5. Finished and consistent: no placeholder, debug label or engine default is on screen in the shipped game; every state (empty, locked, unknown, selected, disabled) is drawn; no mark of image generation is left (melted or mushy forms, meaningless glyphs, line weight that changes within one object); the same thing always looks, moves and sounds the same; one meaning per color or mark; text breaks only between words (a Korean line never ends inside a word, such as `못했` over `다`); no word is cut by the screen edge, and a word stays readable over what runs behind it; a number or letter built from parts (segments, strokes, tubes) has its parts joined and aligned; a field behind text is drawn on purpose, the same on every line of its kind, not a soft dark smudge sized to each line, which reads as a stain or a rendering fault. Check: apart from places still waiting for their resource, is everything on screen finished art, with no debug label, engine default or generation mark? Check: does every line of text break between words? Check: at full size, is every word whole and readable over what is behind it, every glyph and number whole with its parts aligned, and does no field behind text look like a stain?
6. Moments are staged: an event unfolds in beats (cause before effect, then the result, then the record of it), sized to its importance; routine actions get almost nothing, rare ones get time. Check: across the play captures, does a rare event (a reward, a level clear, a death) get more time and space than a routine one, with its cause seen before its effect?

## UI screens

- The windows, buttons and HUD pieces are laid out and staged as in the presentation game's UI and drawn in the hand of this game's world (its line, shading, palette and motifs, or a thin object of that world such as a page, a card or a banner), or are reduced to plain type over the scene. When the presentation game's UI is drawn in another hand than this game's world (e.g. glossy buttons over a flat world), the world's hand wins. The layout is taken in the presentation game's proportions too: how large the words are against the screen and against what holds them, and how full each sheet or panel is. Small plain type in a large, mostly empty page looks like a form. Check: apart from pause, popups, settings, dialogue, notifications, loading and leaderboards, do the panels and buttons look made for this game, so that they could not be pasted into another game unchanged? Check: are the words and what holds them in the presentation game's proportions, with no small type in a large, mostly empty sheet or panel?
- The world's heavy materials stay in the world unless the presentation game builds its screens from them. Text sits on no container at all (over the scene or a full-screen ground), on a quiet flat or translucent field, or on a thin object of the world (a page, a card, a banner, a smear of ink or soot, a projection of light). A slab of stone, metal or wood is rare, mostly stores, inventories, progress and settings screens. Buttons are words, glyphs, flat shapes (at most a thin rim in the world's palette, e.g. gold or bronze) or the content itself. Raised bevelled or glossy plates are rare on landscape screens; on a portrait phone screen they are a common form of a touch button. Check: does the text sit on no container, a quiet field or a thin object of the world (or on a slab only where the presentation game's same screen is one), with buttons that are words, glyphs, flat shapes or the content (or raised and glossy where the world's characters are, or on a portrait phone screen)?
- Type speaks in the game's voice: the game's name is a drawn logo, and the text is set in a face chosen for this game's world, as the presentation game's screens are lettered in theirs (a hand like the logo's, a serif, small capitals, a pixel face, or a sans set with intent, such as thin, widely spaced capitals), not a neutral UI sans left at its defaults; titles, big numbers and main buttons stand apart from the body text. Pause, popups, settings, dialogue, notifications, loading and leaderboards, and small text on the play scene (HUD numbers and labels, control hints), may stay in a plain face that reads at its size. Check: is the name drawn, and is the text of the main menu, results, game-over, inventory, shop and character screens in a face chosen for this world rather than a neutral UI sans? Check: do titles and big numbers differ from body text?
- A screen that presents one thing (a title, a result, a popup, a moment) has one focal element; settings, inventories, shops, level lists and the HUD need none. Groups are separated by space, lines or a quieter inner panel, not by boxes of the same weight nested in each other; in play the HUD keeps to the edges and leaves the middle clear. Check: where the screen presents one thing, is there one obvious focal element? Check: are there no boxes of the same weight nested in each other?
- The title, the main menu and the lobby are staged like a poster: one image from the game (a place of its world or its characters) carries the screen, and the logo and a short menu sit in its calm space or, over busy detail, stay readable with an outline or shadow; or the drawn logo stands large on a plain ground or pattern of the game's palette. Check: are the logo and menu readable over one image from the game, or does the drawn logo stand large on the game's own ground?
- On a portrait phone screen the main menu is cards, tiles or objects rather than a list of words, and touch controls sit in reach of the thumbs. Check: on a portrait phone screen, are the main menu and the touch controls so?
- Results and rewards lead with the outcome: one word, rank, medal or number stands far above the rest, or the game's characters or a staged image carry the screen, and a short table of the round's few numbers comes under it; a long table is one step away. A result that is only a short table in the game's world is the plain form. The next action (next, retry) is in view, and where the game keeps a best score or a target, the result shows the score against it. A game-over screen leads with one big word or a staged scene, drawn in the game's world and voice (e.g. a diary page in the hero's crayon hand, a merchant's ledger, a decree of excommunication, a cracked crown banner, one brushed character over the hero's body). Check: does a game-over screen lead with one big word or staged scene, and does a result lead with the outcome or stay a short table, in the game's world? Check: on a result, is the next action in view?
- A choice (an upgrade, a card, a reward) shows each option's name, a picture and one line of what it does. Check: can the player tell what each option does without opening anything?
- A popup or overlay is made in the game's language (a box drawn in the world's line and palette, lettering and a hairline over the dimmed game, a constellation line, the card itself, a thin object of the world) or is plain type over the live game; never a generic dialog box. Check: does the popup look like it belongs to this game rather than to an app?
- Every other screen is made the same way: dialogue as a balloon, scroll, comic caption, a box with the speaker's portrait or a bare line over a soft band in the world's line; a tutorial as one instruction in one sentence over the live game, at the screen edge, beside the thing it explains or drawn into the scene, with the glyph of the real key or button; a map as a flat schematic in the game's palette or an object of the world (a chart, a picture-book page, a diorama, a relief), with the player's place marked; stats and character sheets as records of the world (a printout, a journal page, a poster, a stage); settings, an inventory and a shop need no built object: plain rows or a grid in the game's palette and type, every current value, price and owned currency in view, with a panel for the selected item where items have detail; a character select with the chosen one shown large; an unlock or achievement as a small toast with an icon at a screen edge over the live game; a loading screen as an image of the game, or a quiet ground with a small indicator; a menu of plain words or flat shapes takes its identity from the image of the game it sits on. Check: is each screen an object or voice of this game's world, plain type over the game's own image, or plain rows or a grid in the game's palette and type?
- Controls are glyphs of the real buttons or keys next to a verb, or a pointer on the exact thing; never sentences of plain text. Check: are controls glyphs or pointers rather than text?
- Focus and state read at a glance: the focused item and every current setting are visible, and destructive or final actions look different. Check: can you tell what is focused and how each option is set?

## characters

- The player and every enemy separate from the ground at play size by a cue the ground does not use. Check: shrunk to a thumbnail, can you point at the player and every enemy?
- Designed for their size: big shapes, a clear silhouette, a signature feature. Check: at in-game size, can you name the head, body and signature feature?
- Grounded and lit by the scene: feet meet the floor, with a shadow where the floor is visible. Check: does each character have a shadow or clear contact, lit like its surroundings?
- Who is who is coded one way: the player singled out, allies and enemies split, bosses set apart, the player's shots unlike the enemies'. Check: without text, can you tell player, enemies, boss and whose shots are whose?
- Each main character has a hook from the game's theme, repeated in every depiction. Check: can you name a feature of the hero and each enemy that comes from this game, not the genre?

## environment

Where play happens in a place (rooms, levels, a map, a table). On a fixed board the theme lives in what surrounds the board while the field stays calm; on an abstract field (e.g. slabs over a gradient) only the play surface check applies.

- Surfaces are authored, not stamped: wear, patches and dressing break up floors and walls so nothing visibly repeats, within the floor's own value range. Check: are the floors and walls the player moves over free of an obviously repeating texture tile (a board's grid of cells is not one)?
- The play surface is the quietest layer: actors, pickups and hazards separate from it by lightness, hue or a mark the surface does not use. Check: does everything the player tracks separate from the surface behind it by lightness, hue or such a mark?
- The space reads as a volume: depth planes, or walls and edges with visible height. Check: are there two depth planes, or walls or edges drawn with height?
- The place is the theme's own: a crypt reads as that crypt, a tavern as that tavern, not as a genre default. Check: when the theme names a place, can you tell it is that place?
- Play geography is drawn into the world: where to walk or place and what blocks. Check: without UI, can you tell where you can go or place?
- Light has a source and a job: it sits objects down and leads the eye to the play space; a flat-lit style does the same with value. Check: do light and shadow, or value in a flat-lit style, sit objects down and lead the eye to the play space?

## props, pickups, effects and icons

- Same hand as the world and the characters. Check: next to the main character, does each prop, effect and icon share its edges, shading, palette and detail?
- Icons of one role form one family: one render, angle, light, outline rule and backing. Check: do two icons of the same role look made in one sitting?
- Every icon and pickup reads at its real size: one subject, a bold silhouette, a hue unlike its neighbours. Check: at native resolution, can you tell what each icon is without a label?
- What must be noticed is the highest-contrast thing in its area; dressing stays quiet. Check: can you find every pickup and projectile within a second?
- Effects are designed shapes with a core, an edge and secondary parts, sized by the event's weight. Check: does each effect have a shape, and across the play captures, is the largest effect on the most important event?
- Danger and targeting are crisp shapes in a color reserved for that meaning. Check: is every danger area a crisp shape in a color nothing else uses?
- Where play happens in a place, the space is furnished in layers: obstacles, breakables and dressing; each prop's look tells what it does. Check: where play happens in a place, is it more than a bare floor or one stamped object?

## accessibility

Automated checks decide what a tool can decide: the option entries of [`client-content`](../client-content/SKILL.md) and the text size, contrast and touch target rules of `## UI quality` of [`ogw`](../../tools/ogw.md). What follows needs a look at the game.

- Color is never the only carrier of a meaning: a state, a side, a pickup, a hazard or a rarity that color tells apart is also told apart by a shape, a mark, an icon or text, so that a player who does not tell those colors apart reads it the same. Check: is every meaning that color carries on the screen also carried by a shape, mark, icon or text?
- Sound is never the only carrier of what the player must react to: an attack about to land, a timer running out, a threat off the screen or a turn passing also shows on the screen, so that a player who plays without sound misses nothing. Check: does every event the player must react to show on the screen, not only in the sound?

## motion and feedback

- Feedback is budgeted by consequence: a routine hit gets a short tint on the target only; kills, damage to the player, rewards and deaths get more; the loudest tools (shake, full-screen flash, long sequences) are kept for rare moments. A routine hit does not shake the screen
- Actions are cut into readable beats: wind-up or contact, result, then cleanup; the cause is seen before the effect; a destruction is a short sequence, not a deletion
- Each motion has a curve that fits its job: fast travel and a held result, entrances that land and short exits; one shared timing grid rather than one default tween everywhere
- Things come from a place that explains them and go where they belong: a panel grows from what opened it, a reward flies to its counter, and the counter answers
- Simple drawings stay alive through squash, stretch, inertia and secondary motion; an idle scene stays calm and has elements that move or animate while there is no input for 2000 ms, and surfaces meant for reading stay still
- The world keeps a record of play and shows progress (stains, debris, a changing tower or castle), not only a number

## sound

An agent cannot hear, so it measures what it can, and a human's ears decide: `ogw resource finalize` matches the loudness and true peak of each sound and gives them, and a human judges the sound by ear in a build and gives feedback with `update game`.

- The sound of this game's thing: an effect is the sound of what the thing is in this game's fiction and mood (the spirit's bolt of light, a shadow scattering, a coin picked up), not a stand-in from another fiction that is heavier or more real (a crossbow for a bolt of light, a sack of ash for a scattering shadow, a bronze coin slapped on a table for a coin pickup, a key dropped in a pouch for every pickup), even when the stand-in has more weight, more power between 250 Hz and 4 kHz and no single tone. A version that stays close to the game's source and is only firmer is better (a hit, a button press)
- Same thing, same sound: the variants of a repeated effect are takes of one sound (one source, material and action), so that repetition reads as one thing
- Budgeted by consequence: routine effects are short and moderate (`level=quiet` in `spec` of their row in `resources.csv`; e.g. a routine hit that peaks within 20 ms and stands about 10 LU over the music for 70 to 80 ms, about 3 a second); kills, rewards and harm to the player get more, and the loudest sounds are kept for rare events (`level=loud`)
- The music is a bed in the game's mood that does not drop out during play and leaves room above its bass for the effects

## what comes from the game, not from this definition

Whether windows have frames at all; which thin object of the world, if any, the UI is made of; frame line and ornament; corner shapes; opaque, flat or translucent panels; fonts and case; the palette and its accent; where the menu and the HUD pieces sit; screen density; diegetic or overlay UI; outlines or none; proportions; camera and projection; whether the grid shows; the value key; rendering technique; amount of atmosphere; how pickups are signalled; color meanings; motion timing and curves; how much glow; the instruments, genre and tempo of the music and the sources of the effects. Each is taken from the game's theme and its reference games.
